# Unexpected Recipes

**Purpose:** Build a Captain Cranberry social episode around an unexpected but genuinely credible recipe or food use for a selected product — expanding what people think the product can do, through use rather than advertising.

**When to run:** Creating an episode for the Unexpected Recipes series.

**Inputs:** Selected product and its real capabilities; recipe direction; web research for recipe legitimacy; product reference image before asset creation.

**Output:** Scored recipe shortlist → developed concept (hero moment, story, copy) → video prompt or per-scene carousel prompts, plus the final package (recipe, product adaptation, on-screen text, caption, hashtags).

**Skills called:** none

**Version:** 4.3

---

# CAPTAIN CRANBERRY — UNEXPECTED RECIPES PROTOCOL
## Version 4.2

---

# 0. PURPOSE

Create a recurring Captain Cranberry social content series built around **unexpected but genuinely appropriate recipes or food uses for a selected Captain Cranberry product**.

The goal is to expand people's perception of what the product can do.

The ideal audience reaction is:

> **"Wait... you can make THAT with this?"**

or:

> **"I never thought of using it that way."**

This is **not**:

- fusion for the sake of fusion
- a random food-hack series
- a shock-value recipe series
- an excuse to force the product into unrelated food content

Every idea should be grounded in a real recipe, real preparation method, or credible culinary use.

The product should demonstrate its versatility **through use**, not through advertising.

---

# 1. CORE PRINCIPLE

## Recipe first. Product fit second. Content third.

Start with:

> **What would actually be interesting and delicious to make?**

Then ask:

> **Why does this Captain Cranberry product make sense for it?**

Only after both are answered should the idea become content.

Do not begin with:

> "How do we feature the product?"

The product should earn attention naturally through what it does.

---

# 2. PROTOCOL STRUCTURE

Every episode moves through three phases:

## PHASE 1 — DISCOVERY & RESEARCH
Choose the product, research genuine recipes, and select the strongest concept.

## PHASE 2 — CREATIVE DEVELOPMENT
Turn the selected recipe into a clear and compelling Captain Cranberry post.

## PHASE 3 — ASSET CREATION
Create either a video or carousel/photo post using authentic social-media visual language.

Proceed interactively.

Do not skip ahead unless the user specifically asks to.

Ask one meaningful decision at a time.

- Thermal mass

- Mold shape

- Material properties

Only use real capabilities.

Never invent a benefit just because it helps the concept.

If the user has supplied product specifications or a product image, treat those as authoritative.

# 7. RESEARCH OBJECTIVE

At this stage, act as:

- Food researcher
- Culinary researcher
- Cultural researcher
- Home cook
- Social content strategist

The goal is **discovery**, not asset creation.

Research genuine recipes or food uses.

Use current web research whenever it helps verify:

- Recipe legitimacy
- Origins
- Traditional preparation
- Established ingredient combinations
- Common variations
- Current food trends
- Product compatibility

Do not rely entirely on memory if research would materially improve accuracy.

---

# 8. RESEARCH REAL RECIPES

Prioritize ideas that:

- already exist
- have established cooking methods
- are genuinely eaten or consumed
- are realistic for home preparation
- make credible use of the selected product
- contain an interesting visual or sensory transformation
- are recognizable enough for the unexpected product pairing to matter

Do not manufacture novelty through random combinations.

### Weak

> Kimchi carbonara taco lasagna in a dolsot.

The novelty comes from stacking unrelated ideas.

### Stronger

> French onion soup in a Korean dolsot.

The recipe is familiar.

The unexpected element is the cookware.

---

# 9. RECIPE SELECTION CRITERIA

Evaluate each candidate across these dimensions.

## 9.1 Authenticity

Ask:

> Is this a real recipe or established preparation?

Avoid ideas invented solely for content.

---

## 9.2 Surprise Factor

Ask:

> Would most viewers normally associate this recipe with the selected product?

The pairing should create genuine curiosity.

---

## 9.3 Product Compatibility

Ask:

> Does the selected product contribute something useful or interesting?

Potential benefits may include:

- Heat retention
- Crisping
- Browning
- Sustained bubbling
- Freezing
- Chilling
- Shape
- Portioning
- Texture development
- One-pot convenience
- Presentation
- Table service
- Controlled pouring

The selected product does not need to be objectively better than every traditional tool.

It simply needs to be a **credible and interesting way to make or serve the recipe**.

---

## 9.4 Visual Potential

Look for natural visual moments such as:

- Bubbling
- Sizzling
- Browning
- Melting
- Crisping
- Caramelization
- Steam
- Sauce thickening
- Egg setting
- Crust formation
- Pouring
- Freezing
- Unmolding
- Condensation
- Colour transformation
- Breaking through a surface
- Scooping through layers
- Clean frozen release
- Chilled drink reveal

Do not manufacture spectacle where none exists.

---

## 9.5 Home Practicality

Ask:

> Would someone realistically recreate this at home?

Avoid concepts requiring:

- Restaurant-only equipment
- Extremely difficult technique
- Impractical ingredient sourcing
- Excessive preparation
- Unrealistic product use

---

## 9.6 Social Value

Ask:

> Would someone have a reason to save, share, comment on, or try this?

Useful surprise is stronger than random novelty.

---

# 10. RECIPE SCORING

Score each candidate:

| Criterion | Score |
|---|---:|
| Surprise Factor | /10 |
| Authenticity | /10 |
| Visual Satisfaction | /10 |
| Product Compatibility | /10 |
| Ease of Recreation | /10 |
| Save / Share Potential | /10 |

**Total: /60**

Use the score as support, not as an automatic decision-maker.

A high numerical score should not override an obvious conceptual weakness.

### Why the Product Makes Sense

### Difficulty
Easy / Medium / Advanced.

### Approximate Time
Realistic home-preparation time.

### Score
__/60

Rank the strongest concepts first.

Wait for the user to select one.

# 13. ADAPT FOR THE SELECTED PRODUCT

Determine what needs to change when using the selected Captain Cranberry product.

Only adapt where necessary.

Depending on the product, evaluate:

- Preheating
- Heat level
- Cooking time
- Cooling time
- Freezing time
- Ingredient sequence
- Liquid level
- Oil or fat
- Lid usage
- Carryover cooking
- Portion size
- Release / unmolding
- Serving temperature
- Texture
- Final presentation

Never change the recipe just to make it seem more product-specific.

# 14. DEFINE THE CREATIVE IDEA

Before any asset generation, answer:

### Why is this recipe unexpected for this product?

### Why does the selected product genuinely make sense?

### What is the "Wait... really?" moment?

### What is the most satisfying visual payoff?

### What should viewers understand about the product without being told directly?

### Why would someone save this?

### Why might someone share it?

### What should someone remember after seeing it?

Keep the concept focused around **one primary idea**.

---

# 15. DEFINE THE HERO MOMENT

Every episode should have one main **Hero Moment**.

This is the visual payoff the post builds toward.

Examples:

- Spoon breaking through a crispy crust
- Cheese bubbling against hot stone
- Crispy rice lifting from the bottom
- Steam escaping when a lid is removed
- Egg yolk breaking
- Sauce bubbling around the edges
- Frozen popsicle releasing cleanly from its mold
- Coffee cube melting into a drink
- Condensation appearing on a chilled glass
- Skull-shaped whiskey stone dropping into a drink
- Caramelized surface being revealed

The hero moment must genuinely arise from the recipe.

Do not invent one purely for spectacle.

---

# 16. DEVELOP THE STORY

Build a simple narrative around the food experience.

A typical structure may be:

### Hook
Create curiosity.

↓

### Setup
Establish what is being made.

↓

### Making
Show genuine preparation.

↓

### Transformation
Something visibly or texturally changes.

↓

### Hero Moment
Deliver the strongest payoff.

↓

### Ending
Finished dish, serving, drinking, or eating moment.

This is a flexible structure.

Do not force every episode into identical pacing.

---

# 17. PRODUCT INTEGRATION

The product should be important without behaving like an advertisement.

Avoid:

- Holding the product toward camera
- Artificial product rotation
- Forced branding shots
- Product-specification callouts
- Beauty shots unrelated to use
- Interrupting the recipe to explain features

Instead, demonstrate benefits through action.

### Example

Instead of:

> "Excellent heat retention."

Show:

> The food still bubbling when the dolsot reaches the table.

Instead of:

> "Creates crispy textures."

Show:

> A spoon scraping through the browned bottom.

Instead of:

> "Keeps drinks cold without dilution."

Show:

> The drink poured over chilled steel stones with no natural ice present.

**Show the benefit rather than announcing it.**

# VISUAL PHILOSOPHY — AUTHENTIC SOCIAL PHOTOGRAPHY
# ============================================================

# 21. CORE VISUAL PRINCIPLE

The visual objective is not simply:

> "Make it realistic."

The objective is:

> **Make it feel like genuine social photography or video captured by a real person during an everyday cooking or food moment.**

Think:

- TikTok cooking posts
- Instagram Stories
- Personal Instagram posts
- Casual dinner photos
- Personal food albums
- Photos sent between friends
- Weekend cooking
- Everyday meal documentation

Do not default to:

- Cookbook photography
- Recipe website photography
- Commercial food advertising
- Amazon product photography
- Restaurant marketing
- Editorial food photography
- Artificial influencer perfection

---

# 22. CORE REALITY TEST

Before finalizing any visual concept or generation prompt, ask:

> **"Does this look like a real photo or video someone casually took while cooking dinner, rather than content created to explain a recipe?"**

This is the governing visual test.

If the answer is no:

Revise.

---

# 23. AUTHENTICITY IS NOT THE SAME AS CANDID

Do not assume authentic content must always look:

- Messy
- Handheld
- Accidental
- Documentary-style
- Mid-action

Real people deliberately photograph food too.

Therefore all of these are allowed:

- Flat lays
- Overhead shots
- Finished food photographs
- Ingredient photographs
- Table photographs
- Close-ups
- Cooking-action shots
- Over-the-shoulder shots
- Handheld video
- Static phone shots

The question is not:

> "Is this candid?"

The question is:

> **"Would a real person naturally take this photograph or video?"**

---

# 25. CAMERA LANGUAGE FOLLOWS INTENT

Do not prescribe camera angles just because they look attractive.

Choose the framing because it makes sense for the moment.

Good uses include:

- Frozen ingredients
- Drink preparation

> Someone saw everything sitting on the counter and thought it looked nice enough to photograph.

Not:

> A professional stylist carefully arranged a recipe diagram.

Avoid:

- Perfect ingredient spacing
- Rigid grids
- Every utensil aligned
- Excessive symmetry
- Ingredients positioned only to explain the recipe
- Artificial negative space reserved for text
- Product perfectly centered purely for advertising

# 26. NATURAL IMPERFECTION

Do not deliberately make scenes messy just to signal authenticity.

Instead allow believable imperfection.

Examples:

- Uneven chopping
- Small sauce marks
- Spoon beside the pot
- Used chopping board
- Open seasoning bottle
- Kitchen towel
- Ingredient packet
- Water droplets
- Small crumbs
- Slight splashes
- Naturally imperfect food placement
- Half-used ingredients

These details should make contextual sense.

Never add random clutter just to make an AI image look less artificial.

---

# 27. PEOPLE

If people appear, they are participating in the food experience first and appearing in content second.

They may:

- Stir
- Chop
- Reach
- Pour
- Taste
- Season
- Carry the product
- Eat
- Talk
- Check doneness
- Retrieve something from the fridge
- Clean while cooking

Avoid default AI behaviours such as:

- Looking into camera
- Holding ingredients up
- Presenting cookware
- Pointing at food
- Frozen smiles
- Influencer poses
- Exaggerated reactions

Unless the concept genuinely calls for it.

---

# 28. LIGHTING

Lighting should match the actual situation.

Examples:

### Breakfast
Natural morning light.

### Lunch
Daylight from nearby windows.

### Dinner
Normal kitchen lighting, window remnants, or warm household ambient light.

### Late Night
Believable indoor artificial light.

Do not automatically use:

- Golden hour
- Dramatic rim lighting
- Cinematic teal/orange grading
- Moody shadows
- Perfectly diffused studio light
- Commercial food lighting

Prioritize:

> **Believability before beauty.**

---

# 29. FOOD ACCURACY

Food must behave correctly.

Maintain realistic:

- Ingredient proportions
- Cooking sequence
- Doneness
- Sauce consistency
- Liquid level
- Steam
- Melting
- Browning
- Freezing
- Condensation
- Texture
- Food volume
- Ingredient transformation

Never sacrifice culinary logic for visual spectacle.

# 30. VIDEO OBJECTIVE

For video, show the making process in a way that feels like someone genuinely captured their cooking experience.

The objective is not to create a polished commercial.

It also does not need to behave like a full instructional recipe video.

The viewer should understand enough of the process to:

- Follow the transformation
- Understand the unexpected use
- Appreciate the product
- Reach the hero moment

---

# 31. DETERMINE VIDEO STRUCTURE

Determine the minimum number of shots or scenes needed.

Do not add shots merely for visual complexity.

Prioritize:

- Immediate curiosity
- Clear cooking actions
- Natural progression
- Visible transformation
- Product use
- Hero moment
- Finished payoff

---

# 32. VIDEO ACTION MUST FEEL REAL

Characters should actually perform meaningful actions.

Avoid sequences where people barely move just because the tone is "natural."

Use believable actions such as:

- Walking into frame
- Reaching
- Opening packaging
- Washing
- Chopping
- Pouring
- Stirring
- Flipping
- Seasoning
- Checking
- Tasting
- Serving
- Eating
- Cleaning as they go

Movement should feel purposeful, loose, and unperformed.

---

# 33. VIDEO COVERAGE

Use camera variation only when it naturally improves the story.

Possible coverage:

- Wide establishing shot
- Medium cooking shot
- Close-up of hands
- Ingredient detail
- Over-the-shoulder
- Top-down moment
- Tight texture shot
- Table payoff

Do not mechanically use:

> Wide → Medium → Close-up → Wide → Medium → Close-up

Let the recipe and action dictate coverage.

---

### Product Use

### Food Behaviour

### Editing / Coverage

Natural finish.

# 38. GENERATE THE CAROUSEL ONE SCENE AT A TIME

After deciding the storyboard:

First present the overall scene sequence.

Then begin with:

## Scene 1

Briefly explain:

- What we see
- Why someone would naturally photograph this moment
- Camera approach
- What the scene contributes to the story

Then create or provide the image-generation prompt for Scene 1.

Wait for the user's feedback.

The user may:

- Approve
- Request changes
- Regenerate
- Change angle
- Change action
- Change visual intent

Only after Scene 1 is resolved should you continue to Scene 2.

Repeat until the carousel is complete.

Do not generate the entire carousel at once unless the user specifically asks.

---

# 40. AUTHENTIC SOCIAL PHOTO TEST

> **If this appeared on someone's TikTok, Instagram, Reddit, Facebook, or personal photo album with no explanation, would I believe a real person captured it?**

---

# 41. CASUAL COOKING TEST

Ask:

> **Does this look like a real photo someone casually took while cooking dinner, rather than a photo created to explain a recipe?**

If no:

Revise.

---

# 42. INTENT TEST

Ask:

> **Why did this person take this photograph or video?**

There should be a plausible human reason.

If the only answer is:

> "Because the carousel needed another image."

Remove or redesign the scene.

---

# 43. RECIPE TEST

Ask:

> **Does the food look and behave like the actual recipe?**

If no:

Revise.

---

# 44. PRODUCT ACCURACY TEST

Ask:

> **Does the selected product accurately match the supplied reference image?**

Check:

- Shape
- Scale
- Geometry
- Material
- Finish
- Colour
- Construction

If no:

Revise.

---

# 45. PRODUCT-USE TEST

Ask:

> **Is the product being used in a way that makes real-world sense?**

If no:

Revise.

Never allow the product to do something it cannot realistically do.

---

# 46. ADVERTISING TEST

Ask:

> **Does this feel like someone is trying to sell me the product?**

If yes:

Reduce product staging.

The product should be noticed through natural use.

---

# 47. STAGING TEST

Ask:

> **Does anything appear positioned mainly for the camera?**

If yes, determine whether a real social-media user would reasonably do the same.

A deliberate dinner flat lay can pass.

A perfectly symmetrical ingredient diagram probably does not.

---

# 48. BEAUTY TEST

Do not ask:

> "Is this polished enough?"

Ask:

> **"Is this believable enough that the beautiful parts feel real?"**

Authenticity takes priority over polish.

# 50. SERIES VARIETY

Across episodes, avoid repeatedly using:

- Same cuisine
- Same ingredient
- Same hero moment
- Same visual structure
- Same camera angle
- Same hook
- Same emotional tone
- Same content format

The **Unexpected Recipes** identity should come from the premise and attitude rather than rigid visual consistency.

One episode might be:

- Casual handheld video

Another:

- Flat-lay-led carousel

Another:

- Quiet weekend cooking sequence

Another:

- Tight close-up food transformation

Another:

- Simple overhead one-pot preparation

Consistency should come from:

> **Curiosity + credible unexpectedness + natural product use + authentic social visuals.**

# 51. SERIES SUCCESS TEST

A successful Unexpected Recipes post should ideally trigger three reactions.

### Reaction 1 — Curiosity

> **"Wait... what's being made?"**

### Reaction 2 — Surprise

> **"You can make that with this?"**

### Reaction 3 — Desire

> **"I actually want to try that."**

If all three happen, the underlying product proposition becomes implicit:

> **"This product can do more than I thought."**

That is the purpose of the series.
